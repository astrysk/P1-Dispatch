# Dispatch — Phase 8: Go-to-Market & Launch Strategy

**Status:** Go-to-market strategy and launch framework defined; launch execution, customer acquisition performance, and market traction require real-world evidence.  
**Baseline:** Dispatch Full-Stack Prototype — Phase 6 Core Validation Complete; Phase 7 Business Model & Commercial Strategy Defined.  
**Reference:** Phase 7 Business Model & Commercial Strategy; Phase 6 Testing, Validation & Quality Assurance; Phase 5 Engineering & Implementation; Dispatch customer discovery and commercial validation requirements.

---

## Phase 8 objective

Define how Dispatch enters the market, reaches its initial manufacturer customers, converts qualified prospects into paying accounts, and establishes a repeatable customer acquisition and onboarding process.

Phase 7 establishes the proposed business model, customer profile, positioning, pricing hypotheses, and unit-economic framework. Phase 8 translates those decisions into an actionable go-to-market strategy covering market entry, customer acquisition, sales execution, pilot recruitment, onboarding, launch planning, distribution channels, and commercial performance measurement.

The objective is not simply to announce or launch the product. It is to establish a repeatable process for acquiring the right customers, getting them to operational value, converting them into paying accounts, and learning from actual market behavior.

The go-to-market strategy must remain evidence-led. Channel selection, sales conversion assumptions, launch targets, acquisition costs, and revenue expectations must be treated as hypotheses until supported by actual prospecting, customer conversations, pilot activity, paid conversions, and operating data.

**A product launch is an event. Go-to-market is the repeatable system that turns a defined market problem into customer adoption and sustainable revenue.**

## 8.1 Go-to-market strategy

### 8.1.1 GTM objective

The initial go-to-market objective is to introduce Dispatch to a narrowly defined group of manufacturers with recurring outbound pickup coordination problems.

The first commercial motion should prioritize learning and customer fit over broad reach. Dispatch should identify manufacturers that experience meaningful pickup uncertainty, rely on external transporters, and have a decision-maker willing to test a structured operational workflow.

The initial GTM strategy should answer five questions:

1. Which manufacturers experience the problem most frequently and materially?
2. Who has the authority and motivation to purchase a solution?
3. How can Dispatch reach and engage those buyers efficiently?
4. What evidence will convince a customer to adopt and pay?
5. Can the acquisition, onboarding, and delivery process be repeated profitably?

### 8.1.2 Initial market-entry approach

The proposed market-entry approach is a focused, founder-led or sales-led B2B motion supported by direct customer discovery and operational pilots.

The initial sequence is:

`IDENTIFY → QUALIFY → DISCOVER → DEMONSTRATE → PILOT → CONVERT → ONBOARD → RETAIN → EXPAND`

| Stage | Purpose | Expected output |
|---|---|---|
| Identify | Find manufacturers matching the target profile | Target account list |
| Qualify | Assess problem frequency, impact, and buying access | Qualified prospect |
| Discover | Understand the current pickup workflow and its failures | Documented problem and baseline |
| Demonstrate | Show how Dispatch addresses the customer's specific workflow | Product evaluation |
| Pilot | Use Dispatch in real pickup operations | Usage and outcome evidence |
| Convert | Move the customer to a paid commercial arrangement | Paying account |
| Onboard | Establish regular operational use | Activated customer |
| Retain | Maintain usage and demonstrate continued value | Renewal |
| Expand | Increase locations, volume, or package scope where justified | Account expansion |

### 8.1.3 Beachhead segment

The initial beachhead should be a focused subset of the Phase 7 ideal customer profile.

Prioritize manufacturers that:

- Dispatch goods regularly.
- Use external transporters or local transport vendors.
- Coordinate pickups through calls, messaging applications, spreadsheets, or fragmented processes.
- Experience recurring delays, non-arrivals, or uncertainty around transporter commitments.
- Can identify the operational impact of pickup failures.
- Have an accessible owner, operations head, or logistics decision-maker.
- Can run a pilot without extensive enterprise procurement or integration work.
- Are willing to share baseline and pilot operating data.

The beachhead should be selected using discovery evidence. Industry labels alone are not sufficient to establish a suitable segment.

### 8.1.4 Market-entry principles

- Start with a narrow customer segment rather than targeting every manufacturer.
- Sell an operational outcome rather than a list of software features.
- Use the customer's existing pickup workflow as the starting point.
- Demonstrate the product against a real customer scenario.
- Keep pilots limited, measurable, and commercially defined.
- Avoid building customer-specific functionality before validating repeat demand.
- Prioritize customers with an identified problem owner and budget authority.
- Measure adoption and outcomes before increasing acquisition spend.
- Expand into adjacent segments only when the initial segment demonstrates repeatable demand.

### 8.1.5 GTM hypotheses

| Hypothesis | Validation method |
|---|---|
| Direct outreach can reach manufacturer decision-makers | Track outreach, response, and meeting conversion |
| Operational discovery creates stronger interest than generic product pitches | Compare discovery-led and feature-led conversations |
| A live pilot reduces adoption uncertainty | Track pilot acceptance and activation |
| Measurable operating outcomes improve paid conversion | Compare baseline and pilot reviews with purchase decisions |
| A simple subscription is easier to purchase | Record pricing objections and approval timelines |
| Standard onboarding can reduce deployment effort | Measure onboarding time across accounts |
| Customers with recurring pickup activity retain better | Compare usage and renewal across customer cohorts |

These are testable assumptions, not established GTM results.

## 8.2 Market entry & target account strategy

### 8.2.1 Target account definition

The target account is a manufacturer that has recurring outbound pickup activity and a meaningful operational dependency on external transporters.

The initial account list should be built from observable qualification signals rather than an unfiltered database of manufacturing businesses.

| Qualification dimension | Information to collect |
|---|---|
| Company profile | Company name, industry, size, and operating model |
| Location | Manufacturing and dispatch locations |
| Dispatch activity | Approximate pickup frequency and operating schedule |
| Transporter model | External transporters, local vendors, or contracted providers |
| Current workflow | Calls, messaging, spreadsheets, ERP, or other systems |
| Problem evidence | Recent delays, missed pickups, or coordination issues |
| Operational impact | Staff time, order delays, penalties, or other consequences |
| Decision-maker | Owner, operations head, or logistics head |
| Buying process | Budget owner, approval process, and procurement requirements |
| Pilot feasibility | Ability to test Dispatch with live pickup operations |
| Data availability | Access to baseline and operational outcome records |

### 8.2.2 Account segmentation

Accounts should be segmented by operational fit and commercial readiness.

| Segment | Characteristics | GTM treatment |
|---|---|---|
| High-priority | Frequent pickup activity, clear failures, measurable impact, accessible buyer | Direct discovery and pilot proposal |
| Medium-priority | Relevant workflow but unclear frequency or financial impact | Further discovery and qualification |
| Low-priority | Infrequent pickups, low impact, or no identified owner | Nurture or deprioritize |
| Enterprise / complex | Multiple locations, formal procurement, integration requirements | Defer until repeatable implementation is established or pursue selectively |
| Poor fit | No meaningful pickup problem or no willingness to change | Disqualify |

### 8.2.3 Initial geographic focus

The first launch geography should be selected using customer density, access to decision-makers, ability to conduct on-site discovery, and operational support requirements.

A geographically concentrated first cohort may reduce travel and onboarding costs and make direct customer support easier.

The chosen geography must be based on actual target-account availability and discovery access. No city or industrial cluster should be treated as validated solely because it has a manufacturing presence.

### 8.2.4 Target account list requirements

Each target account record should include:

- Company name and website.
- Industry and manufacturing activity.
- Operating and dispatch locations.
- Estimated pickup activity, if known.
- Relevant decision-maker and role.
- Contact source and outreach history.
- Qualification status.
- Known current workflow.
- Documented problem evidence.
- Next action and owner.
- Discovery outcome.
- Pilot or commercial status.

Estimated information must be labeled as estimated. Do not present assumed pickup volume, company size, or buying authority as verified facts.

### 8.2.5 Market-entry validation gate

The initial market-entry approach is ready for controlled execution when:

- The beachhead customer profile is documented.
- A qualified target-account list exists.
- Relevant decision-maker roles are identified.
- A discovery script and qualification criteria are ready.
- A product demonstration can be run against the core pickup workflow.
- A pilot scope and commercial process are defined.
- Customer data and feedback can be recorded consistently.

## 8.3 Customer acquisition & distribution channels

### 8.3.1 Acquisition strategy

Dispatch should initially prioritize channels that enable direct access to manufacturer decision-makers and provide high-quality feedback.

The objective is not to maximize leads. It is to acquire qualified conversations with businesses that have the problem, authority, and willingness to adopt a solution.

### 8.3.2 Potential acquisition channels

| Channel | Intended audience | Primary use | Validation requirement |
|---|---|---|---|
| Founder-led outreach | Owners, founders, and business heads | Direct discovery and early sales | Meeting and paid conversion by account |
| Direct sales outreach | Operations and logistics decision-makers | Prospecting and qualification | Qualified opportunity and sales cycle |
| Industrial associations | Manufacturer members and business networks | Introductions and trust-building | Qualified introductions and customer conversion |
| Industrial clusters and local networks | Manufacturers within a defined geography | On-site discovery and pilot recruitment | Cost per qualified account and pilot |
| Referrals | Existing customer and industry relationships | Trust-led acquisition | Referral-to-customer conversion |
| Logistics consultants and service providers | Businesses advising manufacturers | Introductions and potential partnerships | Partner-sourced qualified pipeline |
| ERP and technology partners | Manufacturers using adjacent systems | Integration-led or referral acquisition | Partner activation and revenue contribution |
| Industry events and trade fairs | Manufacturer owners and operations teams | Lead generation and discovery | Qualified meetings and pipeline progression |
| Educational content and search | Buyers researching dispatch and logistics problems | Inbound demand and credibility | Qualified inbound leads and conversion |
| Targeted digital campaigns | Defined manufacturer audiences | Test demand and generate leads | Cost per qualified opportunity and CAC |

Channel inclusion does not mean the channel is proven. Each channel should be tested with a defined budget, audience, message, and conversion measurement.

### 8.3.3 Channel prioritization

The initial channel priority should be based on learning speed, access to buyers, and cost of validation.

**Priority 1 — Direct customer access**

- Founder-led introductions.
- Direct outreach to owners and operations heads.
- On-site discovery with qualified manufacturers.
- Referrals from existing business relationships.

**Priority 2 — Industry access**

- Manufacturing associations.
- Industrial networks and clusters.
- Logistics consultants and service providers.
- Relevant trade events.

**Priority 3 — Scalable acquisition experiments**

- Search-led content.
- Targeted digital campaigns.
- Partner distribution.
- Repeatable outbound sales operations.

Broad paid acquisition should not be scaled before the product demonstrates a clear customer profile, conversion process, retention behavior, and credible unit economics.

### 8.3.4 Channel measurement

Each channel should be measured using:

- Number of target accounts reached.
- Number of responses.
- Qualified discovery meetings.
- Completed discovery conversations.
- Pilot proposals issued.
- Pilots accepted.
- Paid pilots.
- Paid subscriptions.
- Revenue collected.
- Acquisition spend.
- Sales time.
- CAC.
- Renewal and expansion by source.

### 8.3.5 Channel decision criteria

A channel should be expanded when it produces qualified customers at an acquisition cost and sales effort compatible with the unit-economic model defined in Phase 7.

A channel should be revised or stopped when it produces large volumes of unqualified leads, low decision-maker access, weak pilot conversion, or acquisition costs that cannot be supported by customer revenue.

## 8.4 Sales strategy & conversion process

### 8.4.1 Sales motion

The proposed initial sales motion is consultative B2B selling.

The sales conversation should begin with the manufacturer's pickup process and operational problems. Dispatch should be demonstrated only after the team understands the customer's workflow and has identified a relevant use case.

The product should not be pitched as a generic logistics dashboard. The conversation should focus on commitment visibility, pickup failures, recovery coordination, and the operational impact of unreliable collections.

### 8.4.2 Sales process

`PROSPECT → QUALIFY → DISCOVER → PROPOSE → DEMONSTRATE → PILOT → COMMERCIAL REVIEW → CLOSE → ONBOARD`

| Stage | Sales activity | Exit condition |
|---|---|---|
| Prospect | Identify and contact a target account | Relevant contact reached |
| Qualify | Check customer profile and problem fit | Prospect meets qualification criteria |
| Discover | Map current workflow and quantify problem | Problem and buyer documented |
| Propose | Define a relevant solution and pilot scope | Customer agrees to evaluate |
| Demonstrate | Show the workflow using the customer's use case | Product fit assessed |
| Pilot | Run a controlled live operational pilot | Pilot evidence collected |
| Commercial review | Review results and present paid package | Buyer receives a defined offer |
| Close | Complete commercial agreement and payment process | Paying customer confirmed |
| Onboard | Configure account and establish regular use | Customer reaches activation criteria |

### 8.4.3 Discovery-led sales conversation

The sales conversation should cover:

**Current process**
- How are pickups requested?
- Who contacts the transporter?
- How is confirmation recorded?
- How do teams know whether a pickup is on schedule?
- Where are pickup records maintained?

**Problem**
- What happens when a transporter does not arrive?
- How often does the team follow up?
- How are delays identified?
- How are replacement transporters arranged?
- What happens to the order or dispatch plan?

**Impact**
- How much time is spent coordinating pickups?
- What costs or operational consequences result from a failed pickup?
- Which teams are affected?
- How is performance currently measured?

**Buying process**
- Who owns the process?
- Who approves software spending?
- Is there an existing budget?
- What would justify purchasing a solution?
- What is the approval timeline?

### 8.4.4 Product demonstration

The demonstration should follow the customer's actual operating workflow.

Recommended demonstration sequence:

1. Create a pickup request.
2. Assign or identify the relevant transporter.
3. Show transporter commitment confirmation or rejection.
4. Show pickup status monitoring.
5. Demonstrate an at-risk or failed pickup scenario.
6. Initiate and coordinate recovery.
7. Show how the original failure remains traceable.
8. Record the final outcome.
9. Review the operational record and available reporting.

The demonstration must use a realistic scenario and must not imply functionality that is not implemented.

### 8.4.5 Sales qualification framework

A prospect should not advance to a pilot only because the contact is interested.

| Qualification question | Required evidence |
|---|---|
| Is the business a target manufacturer? | Company and operating profile |
| Does it have recurring outbound pickups? | Actual or customer-reported operating pattern |
| Is pickup coordination a material problem? | Recent examples and impact |
| Is there a responsible process owner? | Named role or person |
| Is the economic buyer accessible? | Buying authority identified |
| Can the customer run a live pilot? | Agreed operational scope |
| Can outcomes be measured? | Baseline data access |
| Is there willingness to pay? | Budget discussion or paid pilot commitment |

### 8.4.6 Objection handling

Common objections should be recorded and answered using evidence rather than unsupported claims.

| Objection | Response approach |
|---|---|
| We already use calls and WhatsApp | Understand whether the current process provides reliable commitments, visibility, and outcome history |
| Our transporter handles everything | Test whether the manufacturer has independent visibility when the transporter fails |
| We do not have many pickup failures | Quantify frequency and impact before proposing a paid solution |
| Our ERP already handles dispatch | Identify whether pickup commitment and recovery workflows are covered |
| Our team can manage this manually | Compare manual effort and outcomes with the proposed workflow |
| We do not want another system | Assess onboarding effort and whether Dispatch can fit into the existing operating process |
| The price is too high | Relate price to measured value and review scope; do not discount without understanding the economics |
| Transporters will not use it | Test transporter participation during a limited pilot |
| We need integrations first | Identify whether integration is essential for initial value or can be deferred |

### 8.4.7 Sales pipeline definitions

| Pipeline stage | Definition |
|---|---|
| New | Account identified but not yet qualified |
| Contacted | Initial outreach completed |
| Engaged | Relevant contact has responded |
| Qualified | Customer and problem fit established |
| Discovery completed | Current workflow and impact documented |
| Demonstration completed | Relevant product workflow demonstrated |
| Pilot proposed | Pilot scope and terms shared |
| Pilot active | Customer is using Dispatch in live operations |
| Commercial proposal | Paid offer submitted |
| Negotiation | Scope or commercial terms under discussion |
| Closed won | Paid commercial commitment verified |
| Closed lost | Opportunity declined or disqualified |
| Nurture | Potential fit exists but timing is not ready |

Pipeline value should not be counted as realized revenue. A closed-won account should require a verifiable commercial commitment and the applicable payment evidence.

## 8.5 Pilot recruitment & execution strategy

### 8.5.1 Pilot objective

The pilot is the bridge between product demonstration and commercial adoption.

It must establish whether Dispatch fits the customer's actual pickup workflow, whether users participate consistently, whether the product captures reliable operational data, and whether the customer experiences enough value to consider paying.

A pilot should not be treated as a success solely because users logged in or sample pickups were created.

### 8.5.2 Pilot selection criteria

Select pilot customers that:

- Match the target customer profile.
- Have a recurring pickup coordination problem.
- Can provide access to relevant operational users.
- Can involve at least the necessary transporter participants.
- Are willing to use Dispatch with live pickup activity.
- Can provide baseline information.
- Have a decision-maker involved in the pilot review.
- Are willing to discuss commercial terms before the pilot ends.

### 8.5.3 Pilot scope

Each pilot must define:

| Element | Required definition |
|---|---|
| Customer | Manufacturer and business unit |
| Location | Facility or dispatch location included |
| Users | Business users and operational participants |
| Transporters | Transporters included in the pilot |
| Pickup scope | Pickup types and expected activity |
| Duration | Start date, end date, and review schedule |
| Baseline | Current pickup performance and coordination process |
| Product configuration | Account, roles, and workflow setup |
| Success metrics | Agreed operational and adoption measures |
| Commercial terms | Fee, payment terms, and included services |
| Support | Named support channel and response process |
| Exit decision | Convert, extend with justification, or stop |

### 8.5.4 Pilot operating process

1. Confirm customer eligibility and pilot objectives.
2. Document the existing pickup workflow.
3. Capture baseline pickup and coordination data.
4. Agree on the pilot scope and commercial terms.
5. Configure the customer account.
6. Onboard business users and relevant transporters.
7. Begin using Dispatch with live pickup requests.
8. Review commitments and operational status regularly.
9. Record delayed, at-risk, failed, and recovered pickups.
10. Track usage, support requests, and workflow completion.
11. Compare pilot performance with baseline.
12. Conduct a customer review.
13. Present a paid continuation proposal.
14. Record the conversion, extension, or exit decision.

### 8.5.5 Pilot success metrics

| Metric | What it measures |
|---|---|
| Account activation | Whether the customer completes setup and begins live usage |
| Pickup volume | Number of real pickups managed |
| Workflow completion | Whether pickups progress through the intended workflow |
| Transporter participation | Whether relevant transporters respond and participate |
| Commitment response time | Time taken to confirm or reject a pickup |
| On-time pickup rate | Pickup reliability against the agreed definition |
| Failure rate | Confirmed missed pickups among eligible scheduled pickups |
| Recovery rate | Share of eligible failures successfully recovered |
| Time to detect | Time taken to identify a pickup problem |
| Time to recover | Time between failure identification and recovery milestone |
| Manual coordination effort | Time spent by staff on follow-up and exceptions |
| Active usage | Meaningful product activity by users |
| Customer satisfaction | Customer-reported experience and value |
| Paid conversion | Whether the customer agrees to continue commercially |

Metric denominators and exclusions must be defined before the pilot begins. Unknown and unresolved outcomes must not be counted as successful or missed without sufficient evidence.

### 8.5.6 Pilot outcome categories

| Outcome | Definition | Next action |
|---|---|---|
| Successful | Workflow adopted, data captured, and customer value demonstrated | Present or continue paid subscription |
| Partially successful | Some value demonstrated but specific barriers remain | Identify changes and decide whether an extension is justified |
| Inconclusive | Insufficient usage or data to assess outcomes | Determine whether another controlled test is worthwhile |
| Unsuccessful | Product does not solve a material problem or adoption is not viable | Record reasons and reconsider segment or product |
| Commercially unsuccessful | Operational value exists but customer will not pay at viable terms | Reassess buyer, pricing, value, or business model |

A successful technical pilot does not automatically prove commercial viability.

## 8.6 Customer onboarding, activation & retention

### 8.6.1 Onboarding objective

Onboarding should move a newly acquired customer from commercial agreement to regular operational use with minimal manual effort.

The objective is to help the customer reach a clearly defined first-value event and establish a repeatable usage pattern.

### 8.6.2 Onboarding stages

| Stage | Activities | Completion condition |
|---|---|---|
| Commercial handover | Confirm package, scope, contacts, and responsibilities | Account handover complete |
| Account setup | Configure organization, locations, and authorized users | Account configured |
| Workflow configuration | Establish agreed pickup process and required information | Workflow ready |
| User onboarding | Train business and operational users | Users can perform required actions |
| Transporter onboarding | Introduce relevant transporters to the required workflow | Required participants can respond |
| First live pickup | Create and manage an actual pickup | First live workflow completed |
| Early-use review | Review usage, issues, and user feedback | Activation barriers addressed |
| Regular operation | Establish routine use and review cadence | Customer reaches agreed usage pattern |

### 8.6.3 Activation definition

An account should not be marked active merely because it has been created or a user has logged in.

Dispatch should define activation around meaningful product usage. A proposed activation condition is that the customer:

- Configures the operating location and relevant users.
- Creates real pickup requests.
- Obtains transporter commitment responses.
- Uses the monitoring workflow.
- Completes at least one real operational workflow through the available product capabilities.
- Has a named internal owner responsible for continued use.

The final activation definition should be confirmed using observed customer behavior and the product's actual implemented functionality.

### 8.6.4 Time to value

Track the time between commercial start or onboarding commencement and the customer's first meaningful operational value.

Potential milestones include:

- Account configuration completed.
- First user activated.
- First live pickup created.
- First transporter response recorded.
- First pickup monitored through the workflow.
- First exception handled.
- First operational review completed.

A shorter time to value can reduce onboarding friction, but it must not be optimized at the expense of data quality or correct operational setup.

### 8.6.5 Customer success and retention

Retention should be managed through demonstrated operational value, not periodic check-ins alone.

Customer success activities should include:

- Regular review of pickup usage and outcomes.
- Monitoring inactive accounts and declining usage.
- Reviewing unresolved workflow barriers.
- Tracking support requests and recurring issues.
- Sharing measured operational improvements.
- Reviewing changes in pickup volume and transporter participation.
- Confirming that the original problem remains relevant.
- Preparing renewal discussions before the commercial period ends.

### 8.6.6 Retention and expansion signals

| Signal | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| Regular pickup creation | Product is part of the operating workflow |
| Consistent transporter responses | Operational participants are engaging |
| Repeated monitoring activity | Users rely on Dispatch for visibility |
| Recovery workflow usage | Product is used during operational exceptions |
| Multiple active users | Adoption extends beyond one individual |
| Additional location requests | Potential expansion demand |
| Higher pickup volume | Potential package or usage expansion |
| Renewal at agreed price | Continued willingness to pay |
| Declining activity | Possible adoption or value problem |
| Repeated support dependency | Potential usability or onboarding issue |

Expansion should follow demonstrated value and customer demand. It should not be pushed before the initial account is stable.

## 8.7 Launch strategy & rollout plan

### 8.7.1 Launch approach

Dispatch should use a controlled, evidence-led launch rather than a broad public launch based only on prototype completion.

The initial rollout should prioritize a limited number of qualified manufacturers, live operational usage, direct feedback, and measurable results.

Public launch activity should be aligned with product readiness, customer proof, support capacity, and the ability to onboard new accounts.

### 8.7.2 Launch stages

**Stage 1 — Internal readiness**

Prepare the product, team, sales material, onboarding process, support process, and customer data collection.

Readiness checks:

- Core workflows validated.
- Known limitations documented.
- Customer-facing product demonstration prepared.
- Pilot process and terms prepared.
- Onboarding responsibilities assigned.
- Support and escalation process defined.
- Data collection and privacy requirements reviewed.
- Commercial proposal and billing process prepared.

**Stage 2 — Design-partner or controlled pilot launch**

Introduce Dispatch to a small group of qualified manufacturers.

Objectives:

- Validate the problem in live operations.
- Observe actual product usage.
- Identify workflow and onboarding friction.
- Collect baseline and pilot outcome data.
- Test transporter participation.
- Assess willingness to pay.

**Stage 3 — Initial commercial launch**

Offer a defined paid package to customers whose pilot results support continued use.

Objectives:

- Establish paid customer accounts.
- Measure conversion and sales cycle.
- Validate pricing and packaging.
- Measure onboarding and support costs.
- Establish customer review and renewal processes.

**Stage 4 — Repeatable market launch**

Expand acquisition only after the initial sales and delivery process demonstrates repeatability.

Objectives:

- Increase qualified prospect volume.
- Expand proven acquisition channels.
- Standardize sales and onboarding.
- Improve retention and account expansion.
- Monitor unit economics and service capacity.

### 8.7.3 Launch assets

| Asset | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Product website or landing page | Explain the problem, target customer, product, and contact path |
| Product overview | Provide a concise explanation of Dispatch |
| Product demonstration | Show the actual pickup workflow |
| Customer discovery brief | Guide problem-focused conversations |
| Pilot proposal | Define scope, duration, metrics, and commercial terms |
| Commercial proposal | Present pricing, package, and agreement terms |
| Onboarding guide | Help customers and transporters begin using Dispatch |
| User documentation | Explain implemented workflows |
| Sales presentation | Support structured buyer conversations |
| Customer case study template | Capture evidence from completed pilots |
| Support process | Define how customers report and resolve issues |

Customer logos, testimonials, quantified outcomes, and performance claims must only be used with permission and supporting evidence.

### 8.7.4 Launch messaging

The launch message should focus on the operational problem and the outcome Dispatch is designed to support.

**Core message:**

Dispatch helps manufacturers manage outbound pickup commitments, monitor execution, identify pickup risks, coordinate recovery, and measure pickup outcomes in one structured workflow.

Supporting message themes:

- Know whether a transporter has committed to a pickup.
- Make pickup delays and risks visible.
- Coordinate recovery when a pickup fails.
- Preserve the history of commitments and operational outcomes.
- Understand pickup reliability through operational records.

These statements describe the intended product proposition. Claims of reduced failure rates, saved costs, or improved on-time performance require measured customer evidence.

### 8.7.5 Launch readiness gate

A launch should proceed only when:

- Product scope and known limitations are clear.
- Core workflows are validated.
- Customer onboarding can be completed.
- Support responsibilities are assigned.
- Commercial terms and billing are ready.
- Customer data handling requirements are addressed.
- Pilot or launch success metrics are defined.
- The team can monitor product usage and operational issues.
- Claims in marketing and sales materials are supported by evidence.

A public launch should not be used as a substitute for customer validation.

## 8.8 GTM operations, metrics & performance management

### 8.8.1 GTM operating system

Go-to-market execution requires a consistent operating process across prospecting, sales, pilots, onboarding, customer success, and finance.

Each customer account should have one record containing:

- Account profile and segment.
- Primary contact and economic buyer.
- Current pickup workflow.
- Problem and impact evidence.
- Qualification status.
- Sales stage.
- Demonstration and pilot history.
- Commercial proposal and agreed terms.
- Payment status.
- Onboarding and activation status.
- Usage and outcome data.
- Support issues.
- Renewal and expansion status.
- Next action, owner, and due date.

### 8.8.2 Acquisition funnel metrics

| Metric | Definition |
|---|---|
| Target accounts | Accounts matching the initial target profile |
| Accounts contacted | Target accounts receiving outreach |
| Response rate | Responding accounts divided by contacted accounts |
| Discovery booking rate | Discovery meetings booked divided by contacted accounts |
| Discovery completion rate | Completed discovery meetings divided by booked meetings |
| Qualification rate | Qualified prospects divided by completed discoveries |
| Demonstration rate | Qualified prospects completing a demonstration |
| Pilot proposal rate | Qualified prospects receiving a pilot proposal |
| Pilot acceptance rate | Accepted pilots divided by pilot proposals |
| Paid conversion rate | Paying customers divided by the explicitly defined eligible cohort |
| Sales cycle | Time from first qualified engagement to paid conversion |
| CAC | Acquisition cost divided by new paying customers |

Every conversion metric must define its denominator and cohort. For example, paid conversion from pilots should be calculated from completed eligible pilots, not from all leads.

### 8.8.3 Activation and retention metrics

| Metric | Definition |
|---|---|
| Onboarding completion | Accounts completing required setup |
| Activation rate | Accounts meeting the agreed meaningful-usage definition |
| Time to first value | Time from onboarding start to first meaningful operational use |
| Weekly active accounts | Accounts with meaningful activity during the week |
| Pickup workflow usage | Number of real pickups managed through Dispatch |
| Transporter participation rate | Relevant transporter participants completing required actions |
| Customer retention | Paying accounts continuing into a subsequent period |
| Logo churn | Share of customer accounts lost in a period |
| Revenue churn | Recurring revenue lost from cancellations or reductions |
| Expansion rate | Existing accounts increasing commercial scope |
| Support burden | Support hours or cases per active account |

### 8.8.4 Commercial performance metrics

The GTM dashboard should connect acquisition activity to revenue and delivery economics.

| Metric | Purpose |
|---|---|
| MRR | Measures active recurring revenue |
| ARR | Annualized recurring revenue |
| ARPA | Measures recurring revenue per paying account |
| Collected revenue | Measures actual cash received |
| CAC | Measures acquisition cost |
| Onboarding cost | Measures cost to activate a customer |
| Cost to serve | Measures recurring delivery cost |
| Gross margin | Measures revenue after cost of revenue |
| Contribution per account | Measures account-level contribution |
| CAC payback | Measures acquisition investment recovery |
| Renewal rate | Measures continued willingness to pay |
| Net revenue retention | Measures revenue retention and expansion |

The definitions and formulas must remain consistent with Phase 7.

### 8.8.5 Review cadence

| Review | Frequency | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Prospecting review | Weekly | Target accounts, outreach, responses, and next actions |
| Sales pipeline review | Weekly | Qualification, discovery, demonstrations, proposals, and closures |
| Pilot operations review | Weekly during active pilots | Usage, pickup outcomes, issues, and customer feedback |
| Customer success review | Regularly, based on account needs | Activation, usage, support, and retention risks |
| Commercial performance review | Monthly | Revenue, conversion, CAC, onboarding, and cost to serve |
| GTM strategy review | At defined milestones | Segment, channel, positioning, pricing, and repeatability |

### 8.8.6 GTM decision rules

- Do not increase lead volume when qualification quality is weak.
- Do not scale a channel based only on clicks, impressions, or raw leads.
- Do not count an unpaid pilot as a paying customer.
- Do not count a verbal commitment as collected revenue.
- Do not mark an account activated based only on account creation or login.
- Do not claim customer outcomes without a baseline and defined measurement.
- Do not expand to additional segments before the initial segment shows repeatable demand.
- Do not scale sales faster than onboarding and support capacity.
- Do not discount pricing without recording the reason and commercial impact.
- Do not claim repeatability from one successful customer account.

## 8.9 Go-to-market risks & dependencies

### 8.9.1 GTM risks

| Risk | Potential impact | Mitigation or validation |
|---|---|---|
| Incorrect target segment | Low response and conversion | Refine segment through discovery evidence |
| Weak buyer access | Long or stalled sales cycles | Identify economic buyers early |
| Feature-led messaging | Low relevance to customer problems | Lead with workflow and operating impact |
| Unproven channel economics | Acquisition costs exceed customer value | Test channels with defined cohorts and budgets |
| Low pilot activation | Insufficient operating evidence | Improve onboarding and pilot scope |
| Transporter non-participation | Incomplete workflow and weak product value | Test transporter onboarding and usability |
| Long pilot duration | Delayed commercial decisions | Agree on duration, metrics, and exit date upfront |
| Free-pilot dependency | Interest does not translate into revenue | Introduce paid pilot or explicit conversion terms |
| Custom implementation requests | High delivery cost and low repeatability | Separate core product from paid custom work |
| Weak customer support | Low adoption and retention | Establish ownership and support process |
| Premature paid acquisition | Wasteful spend before conversion is understood | Validate direct sales and unit economics first |
| Unsupported marketing claims | Loss of trust and commercial risk | Require evidence for outcome claims |
| Inadequate operational capacity | Poor customer experience during growth | Scale acquisition in line with delivery capacity |
| Data quality issues | Inaccurate outcome measurement | Define data ownership and validation |
| Product readiness gaps | Pilot disruption and customer dissatisfaction | Use Phase 6 validation and release gates |

### 8.9.2 Dependencies

Phase 8 depends on:

- Phase 7's target customer and proposed business model.
- A sufficiently stable and validated full-stack product.
- Defined pickup workflows and domain states.
- Customer discovery access.
- A working demonstration environment.
- Pilot onboarding and support capacity.
- Commercial terms and payment processes.
- Data collection and reporting capability.
- Clear responsibilities across sales, product, engineering, and customer operations.

If a dependency is not ready, the launch scope should be reduced rather than presenting the product as more mature than it is.

## Phase 8 outcome

Phase 8 establishes how Dispatch will enter the market and build a repeatable path from target-account identification to paid customer adoption.

The proposed initial approach is a focused B2B market entry targeting manufacturers with recurring outbound pickup activity and material coordination problems. Customer acquisition begins with direct discovery and qualified outreach, progresses through workflow-specific demonstrations and controlled operational pilots, and moves to paid conversion only when customer value and willingness to pay can be assessed.

The phase defines acquisition channels, sales stages, pilot execution, onboarding, activation, retention, launch stages, commercial reporting, and the operational dependencies required to support growth.

The go-to-market framework is designed to prevent premature scaling. Broad marketing, large acquisition budgets, and expansion into additional customer segments should follow evidence of target-customer fit, pilot adoption, paid conversion, retention, and viable unit economics.

**Phase 8 completion boundary:** The go-to-market and launch framework is documented. Market traction, acquisition efficiency, paid conversion, retention, and GTM repeatability remain unproven until supported by actual execution and measured customer data.
