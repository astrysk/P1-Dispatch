============================================================
1.1 PROBLEM STATEMENT
============================================================

THE PROBLEM

Small-scale manufacturers need to move finished goods from their factories to distributors, wholesalers, warehouses, other businesses, and end customers.

Many depend on local transporters, independent fleet operators, brokers, or logistics providers to arrange these movements. Problems arise when the agreed vehicle does not arrive on time, a transporter cancels at the last minute, or a replacement is difficult to arrange.

The manufacturer bears the consequences. Goods remain at the factory, dispatch schedules change, employees spend time contacting transport providers, and customer delivery commitments come under pressure.

Finding a vehicle is only part of the job. Manufacturers also need the vehicle to arrive when agreed, or to know quickly when it will not.


THE UNDERLYING PROBLEM

The main gap is between booking a vehicle and having it show up as agreed.

If the manufacturer cannot plan around a booking or find a quick alternative when it falls through, the booking offers little certainty.

1. PICKUP RELIABILITY
   What can go wrong: The vehicle does not arrive within the agreed window.
   Business consequence: Dispatch is delayed.

2. CONFIRMATION
   What can go wrong: The manufacturer cannot establish whether the booking will be fulfilled.
   Business consequence: Planning becomes uncertain.

3. VISIBILITY
   What can go wrong: The transporter provides inadequate or late updates.
   Business consequence: The manufacturer discovers failures too late.

4. ACCOUNTABILITY
   What can go wrong: Responsibility and remedies for missed pickups are unclear.
   Business consequence: The manufacturer must negotiate a resolution.

5. RECOVERY
   What can go wrong: An alternative vehicle cannot be secured quickly at an acceptable cost.
   Business consequence: Delays and additional expenses compound.


HOW THE PROBLEM PLAYS OUT

Consider a small manufacturer that has completed an order of industrial components for a customer.

The goods have been inspected, packed, and prepared for dispatch. The manufacturer books a vehicle for collection the following morning.

The vehicle does not arrive.

The dispatch manager contacts the transporter but receives no reliable arrival estimate. The loading team is waiting, the goods occupy space needed for the next production cycle, and the manufacturer starts contacting alternative providers.

A replacement vehicle is eventually found, but it costs more and arrives later than planned.

The transportation charge is only one part of the loss. The manufacturer has also spent employee time resolving the problem, disrupted its dispatch schedule, and potentially delayed the customer's order.

The cost of a missed pickup extends beyond the cost of the vehicle itself.


WHY THIS PROBLEM MATTERS

India's logistics costs were estimated at approximately Rs. 24.01 lakh crore, equivalent to 7.97% of GDP, for FY 2023-24 in the Government of India's logistics cost assessment.

The Ministry of MSME reports that MSMEs accounted for 36% of manufacturing output in FY 2021-22.

Sources:
- DPIIT - Logistics Cost Assessment:
  https://www.dpiit.gov.in/static/uploads/2025/09/c0d7c33f0b737ed71728ff6c2a35f1e7.pdf
- Ministry of MSME - RAMP Programme:
  https://ramp.msme.gov.in/ramp/about-us/about-ramp

These figures show how important logistics and MSMEs are to the Indian economy. They do not tell us how often factory pickups fail or what those failures cost.

Dispatch needs to find manufacturers that face this problem often and are not getting a satisfactory solution from their current providers.


PROBLEM STATEMENT

Small-scale manufacturers that depend on external transport providers may lack a dependable way to ensure that scheduled outbound pickups happen as agreed. When pickups fail, they must coordinate replacements, absorb additional costs, and manage the consequences for production, dispatch, and customer commitments.

Dispatch aims to address this problem through dependable pickup arrangements, clear accountability, and a practical recovery process.

1.2 MARKET RESEARCH
============================================================

1.2.1 INDIA'S LOGISTICS LANDSCAPE

India's road freight industry includes manufacturers, independent truck owners, small fleet operators, transport companies, brokers, digital freight marketplaces, on-demand transportation services, and integrated logistics providers.

SIDBI's 2025 report, "Understanding Indian MSME Sector: Progress and Challenges", describes road transport as a fragmented industry with a significant role played by small fleet operators. It discusses their dependence on brokers and the limitations of their distribution networks.

Source: SIDBI - Understanding Indian MSME Sector:
https://www.sidbi.in/uploads/publicationreport/Understanding-Indian-MSME-sector-Progress-and-Challenges%20-Unabridged-Version-07-07-2025.pdf

With so many different types of operators involved, coordination matters. But a fragmented market is not automatically an unreliable one; many local transporters serve their customers well. Dispatch needs to find the specific situations where current arrangements fail.


1.2.2 DEFINING THE MARKET

Dispatch's initial focus is narrower than the overall logistics market.

It is concerned with scheduled outbound pickups of finished goods from small and medium-sized manufacturing facilities.

The focus is on businesses that depend on external transportation and need vehicles to arrive within an agreed window.

A manufacturer shipping five loads per month has different requirements from one shipping fifty loads per day. Similarly, a business moving goods within an industrial cluster has different needs from one shipping full truckloads across states.

The target market should be defined by the pickup problem, not company size alone.


1.2.3 POTENTIAL CUSTOMER SEGMENTS

1. SMALL MANUFACTURERS WITH RECURRING SHIPMENTS
   Typical requirement: Regular collection of finished goods.
   Potential problem: Dependence on a limited transporter network.
   Priority: High.

2. MANUFACTURERS WITH FIXED DELIVERY SCHEDULES
   Typical requirement: Collection within a defined time window.
   Potential problem: Missed pickups disrupt delivery commitments.
   Priority: High.

3. MANUFACTURERS USING AD HOC TRANSPORT ARRANGEMENTS
   Typical requirement: Vehicle sourcing for individual shipments.
   Potential problem: Limited predictability and backup capacity.
   Priority: High.

4. MANUFACTURERS WITH DEDICATED CONTRACTED FLEETS
   Typical requirement: Planned, recurring transportation.
   Potential problem: Existing agreements may already provide adequate reliability.
   Priority: Lower initially.

5. MANUFACTURERS SHIPPING OCCASIONAL LOADS
   Typical requirement: Infrequent transportation.
   Potential problem: Insufficient recurring demand to justify a new service.
   Priority: Uncertain.

6. LARGE MANUFACTURERS WITH INTEGRATED LOGISTICS OPERATIONS
   Typical requirement: High-volume, multi-location shipping.
   Potential problem: Established logistics infrastructure and procurement processes.
   Priority: Not the initial focus.


INITIAL CUSTOMER PROFILE

Dispatch should initially focus on manufacturers that:

- Ship finished goods regularly.
- Depend on external transportation.
- Need pickups to happen within specific time windows.
- Have limited or unreliable backup arrangements.
- Face meaningful operational consequences when a pickup fails.
- Can make decisions about their transportation arrangements.


1.2.4 CHOOSING THE INITIAL GEOGRAPHY

A concentrated industrial cluster is a more practical starting point than launching across India.

A pickup-reliability service needs enough transport capacity near its customers to respond when a booking fails. A geographically dispersed network makes it harder to arrange replacements quickly.

Starting in one cluster would make it easier to build manufacturer relationships, learn which vehicles they need, and develop a dependable group of transport providers.

Bengaluru's industrial areas are one possible market to examine. The best location will depend on manufacturer density, shipment patterns, transport availability, and the cost of providing dependable pickups.

MARKET CONCLUSION:
Dispatch should begin with a clearly defined group of manufacturers in a concentrated geographic area.

1.3 USERS AND OPERATING SCENARIOS
============================================================

1.3.1 THE PEOPLE INVOLVED

THE FACTORY OWNER

The owner is accountable for the commercial outcome. A shipment may represent a customer order, an invoice awaiting fulfilment, or a commitment that affects an important business relationship.

Primary concern:
Delivering customer orders without avoidable costs, delays, or reputational damage.

What matters:
- Dependable fulfilment of customer orders.
- Predictable transportation costs.
- Reduced dependence on a single transporter.
- Clear accountability when a booking fails.
- Less management time spent resolving routine dispatch problems.

Adoption barrier:
A new provider must demonstrate that it is more dependable than existing relationships.


THE DISPATCH MANAGER

The dispatch manager coordinates the movement of goods out of the factory. The role involves arranging vehicles, confirming availability, preparing shipment documentation, coordinating loading teams, and communicating with customers or internal departments.

When a vehicle fails to arrive, the dispatch manager often becomes responsible for finding a solution.

Primary concern:
Getting goods out of the factory according to schedule.

What matters:
- Confirmed vehicle availability.
- Accurate pickup status.
- Timely notification of delays.
- A replacement arrangement when the original booking fails.
- Less time spent chasing transport providers.

Adoption barrier:
A service that adds work without improving pickup outcomes will not solve the problem.


THE TRANSPORTER OR FLEET OPERATOR

The transporter's operating economics directly affect whether dependable pickups can be delivered.

An independent operator must balance vehicle availability, driver schedules, route profitability, loading delays, and other commitments.

Primary concern:
Completing profitable trips while keeping vehicles and drivers productively scheduled.

What matters:
- Accurate pickup information.
- Reasonable scheduling commitments.
- Fair payment terms.
- Predictable demand.
- Clear loading requirements.
- Fair treatment when delays are caused by the shipper.

Adoption barrier:
Unreasonable penalties, poor route economics, payment uncertainty, or unrealistic service commitments.


WHY ALL THREE MATTER

The manufacturer needs reliability. The transporter needs a commercially viable arrangement. Dispatch must make both requirements compatible.

A service that improves the manufacturer's experience while consistently creating losses for transport providers will be difficult to sustain.


1.3.2 OPERATING SCENARIOS

These examples show how pickup problems can happen in day-to-day operations.


SCENARIO 1: THE CONFIRMED VEHICLE THAT NEVER ARRIVES

A small manufacturer has completed an order of fabricated metal components for a distributor.

The dispatch manager books a truck for collection at 10 a.m. The transporter accepts the booking, but by 10:30 a.m., the vehicle has not arrived.

The transporter is difficult to reach and cannot provide a reliable arrival estimate. The manufacturer contacts alternative providers and eventually finds a replacement that arrives later than planned.

The goods remain at the factory, the dispatch schedule is disrupted, and the manufacturer spends time finding another vehicle. The booking was accepted, but there was no dependable way to know whether it would be fulfilled or to recover quickly. Dispatch could help with clear confirmation, timely updates, and backup arrangements—if this kind of failure happens often enough.


SCENARIO 2: THE VEHICLE ARRIVES TOO LATE

A manufacturer has arranged a shipment of industrial components for a business customer. The goods must leave the factory early enough to meet the delivery schedule.

The vehicle is scheduled to arrive in the morning, but the transporter delays dispatching it. The manufacturer receives an update only after much of the pickup window has passed.

A replacement vehicle is available, but not early enough to preserve the original schedule. The manufacturer must inform the customer of the delay.

The delay threatens the customer's expected delivery date. The manufacturer did not receive information early enough to make a useful recovery decision. Earlier warnings and faster replacement arrangements could help reduce the knock-on delay.


SCENARIO 3: THE LAST-MINUTE CANCELLATION

A manufacturer has recurring shipments but relies on a local transporter for a particular route.

The transporter confirms a vehicle for the following day but cancels on the morning of collection because the vehicle is unavailable.

The manufacturer contacts other transporters. The available replacement quotes a higher price or offers a later collection.

The manufacturer must choose between paying more and accepting a delay. The original booking offered little protection against cancellation. A more dependable service could be valuable, but only if Dispatch can arrange replacement capacity without making the booking unprofitable.


SCENARIO 4: THE GOODS ARE NOT READY

A transporter is scheduled to collect finished products at 11 a.m. Production or quality inspection runs late, and the goods are not ready when the vehicle arrives.

The transporter incurs waiting time or moves to another job. The manufacturer later needs to arrange another pickup.

The shipment is delayed, and both parties may incur additional costs. The booking was made without a clear understanding of when the goods would be ready. Dispatch would need to confirm shipment readiness, estimate loading time, and make it easy to renegotiate the pickup window.

Dispatch cannot define reliability solely as the transporter's obligation to arrive. It must coordinate the manufacturer's readiness and the transporter's availability.


SCENARIO 5: THE BACKUP VEHICLE COSTS TOO MUCH

A manufacturer needs a shipment to leave on the same day to meet a customer commitment.

The primary transporter cancels. The manufacturer contacts alternative providers, but only a few vehicles can meet the required timing and capacity.

The available providers quote a premium. The manufacturer either pays more or accepts the delay.

The manufacturer faces an unexpected transportation expense because there is no dependable backup at an acceptable price. Planning capacity in advance and building relationships with alternative providers may help, but the cost of keeping those options available needs to make sense.


SCENARIO 6: POOR COORDINATION CAUSES THE FAILURE

A manufacturer works with several local transporters and arranges each shipment through calls and messages.

Different providers give different availability estimates. The manufacturer confirms one vehicle but continues following up because the booking status is unclear.

Loading arrangements change, but the updated information does not reach the driver in time. The pickup is delayed even though transportation capacity was available.

Coordination consumes time and creates avoidable uncertainty because information is spread across the manufacturer, transporter, and driver. Keeping booking details in one place, confirming arrangements clearly, and flagging changes could reduce these failures.


WHAT THESE SCENARIOS REVEAL

Missed pickups can result from insufficient transport capacity, unreliable booking commitments, late communication, inadequate backup arrangements, or poor coordination.

Each cause requires a different response.

Dispatch should not assume every failure can be solved by finding another vehicle. It must identify the underlying problem and respond accordingly.

1.4 USER PERSONAS
============================================================

Dispatch involves three main groups of people, each with different priorities.


FACTORY OWNER

Primary objective:
Fulfil customer commitments while protecting margins.

Typical workflow:
Oversees commercial commitments and escalates major disruptions.

Main frustration:
Unexpected costs and threatened customer relationships.

Decision criteria:
Reliability, price, accountability, and business impact.

Motivation to adopt:
Fewer disruptions and less management intervention.

Reason to reject:
Existing providers work well or the service costs too much.

Evidence of value:
Fewer costly disruptions and more dependable dispatch.


DISPATCH MANAGER

Primary objective:
Move goods according to schedule.

Typical workflow:
Books vehicles, coordinates loading, tracks pickups, and resolves exceptions.

Main frustration:
Repeated calls, uncertain arrivals, and last-minute recovery.

Decision criteria:
Timeliness, visibility, and ease of coordination.

Motivation to adopt:
Less coordination and faster exception resolution.

Reason to reject:
The service adds effort without improving outcomes.

Evidence of value:
Less coordination effort and better pickup performance.


INDEPENDENT TRANSPORTER

Primary objective:
Complete profitable trips.

Typical workflow:
Accepts bookings, allocates vehicles and drivers, and manages routes.

Main frustration:
Unclear shipment readiness, waiting time, and poor route economics.

Decision criteria:
Earnings, scheduling feasibility, payment, and fair operating terms.

Motivation to adopt:
Better-organized bookings and predictable demand.

Reason to reject:
Commitments are unrealistic or economics are unattractive.

Evidence of value:
Better vehicle utilization and commercially viable jobs.


PRIMARY USER AND ECONOMIC BUYER

The dispatch manager is likely to be the primary operational user, while the factory owner or business manager is likely to control the purchasing decision in smaller manufacturing businesses.

The transporter is the critical supply-side participant.

Dispatch therefore operates across three parties:

- The manufacturer creates demand for a pickup.
- The transporter fulfils the pickup.
- Dispatch coordinates the commitment, visibility, and recovery process.

The service must create enough value for manufacturers to pay while making participation commercially worthwhile for transport providers.

1.5 EXISTING SOLUTIONS & COMPETITOR ANALYSIS
============================================================

Transportation booking and freight coordination are already established services. Dispatch will need to show where existing options fall short for its target manufacturers and why customers would switch or use an additional provider.


1.5.1 PORTER

Porter offers on-demand goods transportation, including trucks, tempos, electric vehicles, and two-wheelers for intracity movement. Its enterprise offering serves business transportation needs.

Source: Porter Enterprise
https://porter.in/enterprise-faq

What it addresses:
- Access to intracity transportation.
- Vehicle booking for different types of goods.
- Business transportation workflows.

Implication for Dispatch:
Manufacturers already have access to digital vehicle-booking services. Dispatch must establish whether its target customers have additional requirements around scheduled industrial pickups, recurring arrangements, backup capacity, or recovery when a booking fails.


1.5.2 VAHAK

Vahak operates a transport marketplace connecting load owners with transporters and truck owners.

Source: Vahak Transport Marketplace
https://www.vahak.in/en/marketplace/load

What it addresses:
- Connecting shippers and transport providers.
- Access to a broader transport network.
- Finding vehicles and loads through a marketplace.

Implication for Dispatch:
If manufacturers can already find transporters but cannot reliably plan around pickup commitments, the opportunity may lie in managed fulfilment and exception recovery rather than another marketplace.


1.5.3 DELHIVERY

Delhivery provides logistics services and documents B2B pickup workflows through which businesses request shipment collection from warehouses.

Source: Delhivery B2B Pickup Request
https://help.delhivery.com/docs/b2b-pickup-request

What it addresses:
- Structured shipment collection.
- Logistics network access.
- B2B shipment movement.

Implication for Dispatch:
Some manufacturers may already have an effective solution through established logistics providers. Dispatch must identify the shipments and circumstances in which existing services do not meet the manufacturer's requirements.


1.5.4 LOCAL TRANSPORTERS AND BROKERS

Local transporters and brokers remain important alternatives, even when they do not operate sophisticated software.

They may offer established relationships, knowledge of local routes, flexibility for unusual shipment requirements, and familiarity with customers, loading processes, and payment practices.

These arrangements can work well.

Problems may arise when the manufacturer depends on a small number of contacts, lacks visibility into vehicle availability, or has no effective way to recover from a failed booking.

Dispatch must provide a measurable improvement over these arrangements. A digital interface alone is unlikely to be sufficient.


1.5.5 COMPETITIVE COMPARISON

LOCAL TRANSPORTER OR BROKER
- Finding a vehicle: Usually through existing contacts.
- Scheduling a pickup: Depends on the arrangement.
- Tracking booking status: Calls or messages may be used.
- Recovering from a failed pickup: Often depends on personal contacts.
- Accountability: Depends on the relationship and agreed terms.
- Industrial shipment support: Depends on local capability.

ON-DEMAND TRANSPORTATION SERVICE
- Finding a vehicle: Through a booking service.
- Scheduling a pickup: Depends on the service.
- Tracking booking status: Platform-dependent.
- Recovering from a failed pickup: Depends on the service's policies and availability.
- Accountability: Depends on service terms.
- Industrial shipment support: Depends on available vehicles and service coverage.

FREIGHT MARKETPLACE
- Finding a vehicle: Through a provider network.
- Scheduling a pickup: Depends on the provider.
- Tracking booking status: Platform-dependent.
- Recovering from a failed pickup: Depends on provider availability.
- Accountability: Depends on the marketplace and provider arrangements.
- Industrial shipment support: Depends on available vehicles and routes.

INTEGRATED LOGISTICS PROVIDER
- Finding a vehicle: Through an established network.
- Scheduling a pickup: Depends on the service.
- Tracking booking status: Depends on the provider's systems.
- Recovering from a failed pickup: Depends on network capacity and service terms.
- Accountability: Depends on the agreed service arrangement.
- Industrial shipment support: Depends on network coverage and service capability.

DISPATCH'S INTENDED FOCUS
- Finding a vehicle: Necessary, but not sufficient.
- Scheduling a pickup: Establish a clear pickup commitment.
- Tracking booking status: Provide timely, actionable updates.
- Recovering from a failed pickup: Establish a defined recovery process.
- Accountability: Clarify responsibilities and remedies.
- Industrial shipment support: Focus on a defined manufacturer segment.


COMPETITIVE CONCLUSION

Dispatch should not position itself as another transport marketplace, truck-booking app, or freight broker with a digital interface.

Its potential differentiation is managed pickup reliability for a clearly defined group of manufacturers.

That differentiation must be demonstrated through better pickup outcomes, faster recovery, or lower disruption costs than customers' existing alternatives.

1.6.2 ILLUSTRATIVE COST SCENARIO

Consider a manufacturer with 100 scheduled outbound pickups per month.

For illustration, assume the following:

- Scheduled pickups per month: 100
- Pickups requiring intervention or recovery: 10
- Share requiring a replacement vehicle: 60%
- Average incremental replacement cost: Rs. 1,000
- Staff time spent resolving each incident: 1.5 hours
- Loaded cost of staff time: Rs. 250 per hour

REPLACEMENT-VEHICLE EXPENSE

10 x 60% x Rs. 1,000 = Rs. 6,000 per month.

COORDINATION COST

10 x 1.5 hours x Rs. 250 = Rs. 3,750 per month.

COMBINED COST

Rs. 6,000 + Rs. 3,750 = Rs. 9,750 per month.

This excludes customer penalties, lost orders, inventory costs, production disruption, and reputational damage.

These are example figures, not industry averages or data collected from manufacturers. Actual costs will vary by shipment, route, available alternatives, and the impact of a missed pickup.

This is one way to estimate how much a missed pickup may cost a manufacturer—and how much a better service might be worth.

A manufacturer experiencing one inexpensive incident per quarter may not need a dedicated service. A manufacturer facing repeated failures that threaten important customer commitments may have a stronger reason to pay.


1.6.3 WHAT WOULD ESTABLISH THE OPPORTUNITY?

A strong case for Dispatch would include:

- Recent, documented pickup failures.
- Shipment records showing the frequency of missed or delayed pickups.
- Verified additional transportation and coordination costs.
- Evidence that existing alternatives do not adequately resolve the problem.
- Customers willing to pay for a defined improvement.
- Transporters willing and able to fulfil the required commitments.
- Evidence that the service can improve pickup outcomes without incurring unsustainable costs.

Customers saying reliability matters is not enough. The case is stronger when they can point to repeated failures, show what those failures cost, and agree to pay for a better service.

1.7 OPPORTUNITY ASSESSMENT
============================================================

1.7.1 THE OPPORTUNITY

Dispatch can be developed as a transportation service built around the successful fulfilment of scheduled factory pickups rather than simply the discovery of vehicles.

The service would coordinate the manufacturer, the assigned transporter, and the pickup commitment. It would make booking status visible and establish a response when the original arrangement is at risk.

The proposed value proposition is:

Manufacturers should be able to plan outbound dispatch around a dependable pickup commitment, with clear communication and a practical recovery process when the commitment cannot be met.


1.7.2 POTENTIAL SERVICE CAPABILITIES

1. STRUCTURED PICKUP BOOKING
   Purpose: Capture location, timing, vehicle requirements, cargo readiness, and destination.
   Business value: Reduces ambiguity.

2. EXPLICIT CONFIRMATION
   Purpose: Establish whether a provider has accepted the pickup commitment.
   Business value: Reduces uncertainty.

3. STATUS UPDATES AND EXCEPTION ALERTS
   Purpose: Communicate meaningful changes before they become critical.
   Business value: Gives manufacturers time to respond.

4. TRANSPORTER PERFORMANCE RECORDS
   Purpose: Track fulfilment against agreed commitments.
   Business value: Supports better provider selection.

5. BACKUP-PROVIDER COORDINATION
   Purpose: Find alternatives when a pickup is at risk.
   Business value: Reduces recovery time.

6. CLEAR SERVICE TERMS
   Purpose: Define responsibilities, cancellation rules, and remedies.
   Business value: Establishes accountability.

7. SHIPMENT-READINESS CONFIRMATION
   Purpose: Confirm goods and loading arrangements are ready.
   Business value: Reduces coordination failures.

These are possible service capabilities, not a final software specification.

The initial service could be delivered through manual coordination, calls, messaging, and a basic booking system. Dispatch should first prove that it can deliver reliable pickups using a simple operating process. A more sophisticated platform can come later if the model works.


1.7.3 BUSINESS-MODEL OPTIONS

MODEL A: PER-PICKUP SERVICE FEE

Manufacturers pay a fee or margin for each fulfilled pickup.

Advantages:
- Revenue is tied to actual usage.
- Individual bookings are relatively straightforward to test.
- Suitable for manufacturers with variable shipment volumes.

Risks:
- Revenue fluctuates with shipment volume.
- The fee must cover coordination and recovery costs.
- Failed bookings can consume time without generating revenue.


MODEL B: SCHEDULED-PICKUP RELIABILITY SERVICE

Manufacturers pay a premium for planned collections with defined service commitments.

Advantages:
- Closely aligned with the value of predictable dispatch.
- Suitable for recurring shipments.
- May support stronger customer relationships.

Risks:
- Requires sufficient transport capacity.
- Service commitments can create financial exposure.
- The premium must be justified by measurable improvements.


MODEL C: RECURRING BUSINESS ACCOUNT

Manufacturers pay a recurring fee for managing multiple pickups, transport-provider coordination, visibility, and exception handling.

Advantages:
- Potentially more predictable revenue.
- Encourages recurring usage.
- Supports a deeper operational relationship.

Risks:
- Requires consistent service quality.
- Customers may resist subscriptions when shipment volumes vary.
- The service must offer more value than existing arrangements.


RECOMMENDED STARTING POINT

Begin by testing a per-pickup service with a clearly defined pickup commitment and recovery process.

This provides a straightforward way to assess customer demand, service performance, and operating economics.

A recurring account can be considered once repeated usage and customer value have been established.


1.7.4 THE ECONOMICS OF RELIABILITY

Greater reliability may require additional operational capacity. Monitoring pickups, maintaining backup providers, resolving exceptions, and arranging replacements all incur costs.

A basic way to calculate the contribution from each booking is:

Contribution per pickup = Customer revenue - Transporter payment - Direct operating cost - Expected recovery cost

Consider the following illustrative example:

- Customer charge: Rs. 3,000
- Transporter payment: Rs. 2,500
- Direct coordination cost: Rs. 150
- Expected recovery cost per booking: Rs. 100
- Contribution per booking: Rs. 250

Under these assumptions, the contribution is Rs. 250 per booking, approximately 8.3% of the customer charge.

These figures are hypothetical and do not represent prevailing freight rates or Dispatch's actual economics.

This calculation needs to be made for each booking. Even with paying customers, Dispatch could lose money if cancellations, backup vehicles, and coordination take more than expected.


1.7.5 KEY RISKS

RISK 1: THE PROBLEM IS LESS FREQUENT THAN EXPECTED
Why it matters: Manufacturers may tolerate occasional failures.
Implication: Focus on businesses with recurring, demonstrable pain.

RISK 2: EXISTING PROVIDERS ALREADY SOLVE IT
Why it matters: A new service may offer little additional value.
Implication: Differentiate through measurable outcomes.

RISK 3: BACKUP CAPACITY IS UNAVAILABLE
Why it matters: A commitment cannot be fulfilled without suitable alternatives.
Implication: Build a geographically concentrated supply network.

RISK 4: CUSTOMERS WILL NOT PAY A PREMIUM
Why it matters: Reliability may not justify the additional cost.
Implication: Test paid bookings before scaling.

RISK 5: TRANSPORT ECONOMICS ARE UNFAVOURABLE
Why it matters: Recovery costs may consume the margin.
Implication: Measure contribution after operating exceptions.

RISK 6: FAILURES ORIGINATE WITH THE MANUFACTURER
Why it matters: Unready cargo can cause delays even when the transporter arrives.
Implication: Define responsibilities and readiness requirements.

RISK 7: SERVICE COMMITMENTS CREATE EXCESSIVE LIABILITY
Why it matters: Uncontrolled guarantees can expose Dispatch to disproportionate losses.
Implication: Establish realistic service windows, exclusions, and remedies.


1.7.6 OPPORTUNITY DECISION

There is a reasonable case for testing Dispatch as a pickup-reliability service for small manufacturers.

The opportunity is strongest if three conditions hold:

1. A specific group of manufacturers experiences recurring, costly pickup failures.

2. Existing alternatives do not adequately address those failures.

3. Dispatch can improve reliability at a price customers will pay while maintaining sufficient contribution after operating costs.

If all three conditions hold, Dispatch can move forward with a focused service. If they do not, it should revisit the target customers or the problem before investing heavily in product development.

RESEARCH CONCLUSION
============================================================

Dispatch starts with a straightforward problem: goods are ready to leave the factory, but manufacturers cannot always count on the vehicle booked to collect them.

India's logistics ecosystem is economically significant, MSMEs contribute substantially to manufacturing, and road freight includes many small operators. At the same time, vehicle-booking services, freight marketplaces, and established logistics providers already serve parts of this market.

Dispatch does not need to recreate services that already exist. It needs to determine whether a particular group of manufacturers would benefit from more dependable pickups, timely updates, and a workable backup when a booking fails.

The operating scenarios identify several possible causes: provider cancellations, delayed arrivals, poor visibility, inadequate backup arrangements, and shipment-readiness problems. Each requires a different response.

The commercial opportunity depends on how frequently these problems occur, what they cost manufacturers, whether existing alternatives fall short, and whether Dispatch can improve outcomes without taking on unmanageable operating costs.

This phase has defined the problem, the likely target customers, the existing alternatives, and the main operating risks. The next step is to turn these findings into product requirements and decide what Dispatch must do to make factory pickups more dependable.
